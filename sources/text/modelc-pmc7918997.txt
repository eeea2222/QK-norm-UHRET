Abstract

In the interest of fire prevention, most materials used in the interior construction of manned spacecraft are non-flammable, however, they do produce smoke when overheated. Spacecraft smoke detectors will ideally detect smoke generated by oxidative pyrolysis (such as smoldering) in order to allow the maximum time for the crew to respond before a larger flaming fire develops. An experiment on the International Space Station (ISS) characterized smoke from overheating common spacecraft materials. The following parameters were controlled: heating temperature, air flow past the samples and duration of aging. Two different spacecraft smoke detectors were included in the instrumentation and their performance with different smoke types has been evaluated. Additional equipment in the experiment included a thermal precipitator to sample particles for microscopic analysis upon return to Earth, and three commercial-off-the-shelf real-time instruments to measure particle mass and number concentration, and an ionization detector calibrated to estimate the first moment of the size distribution. Results from the ISS experiment show that smoke particles vary in morphology and average diameter, however, they are not significantly different from smoke particles generated in equivalent experiments performed in normal gravity. The two spacecraft smoke detectors did not successfully detect every type of smoke, which demonstrates that the next generation of spacecraft fire detectors must be improved and tested against smoke from relevant space materials.

Keywords: Smoke, Fire Detector, microgravity

1.0. Introduction

All existing spacecraft smoke detectors have been designed based on fire data from experiments in normal gravity for example as described in Bukowski and Mulholland [1] and Bukowski et al. [2]. Many materials of interest for the terrestrial fire detection community are found in residential and commercial buildings, and represented in standard test fires encompassing smoldering wood, shredded paper and polyurethane foam, and flaming fires of wood, heptane/toluene liquid mixtures and polyurethane foam [3]. This body of knowledge is not directly applicable to spacecraft fire safety for several reasons. First of all, the spacecraft environment is a fixed volume of air with very limited evacuation options. Therefore, a fire must be detected in its very early stages, as materials are being heated beyond their safe use temperatures, making pre-ignition oxidative pyrolysis rather than combustion the relevant mode of smoke generation to characterize. Secondly, in the absence of buoyant flow in low gravity, smoke does not rise to the ceiling, but instead can either concentrate at the source, causing the particles to grow by coagulation as time progresses (referred to as aging) and/or be dispersed throughout the spacecraft by the mixing induced by the ventilation system. The former increases the smoke particle sizes which influences detector performance and the latter rapidly reduces the concentration, making detection particularly challenging. Finally, the materials used in the interior of spacecraft are very different from the Underwriters Laboratory (UL) [3] standard fire fuels. For these reasons, the Smoke Aerosol Measurement Experiment (SAME) was devised to create and characterize smoke in low gravity. This experiment was flown twice on the International Space Station (ISS), in 2007 and 2010, and a series of ground-based tests with the identical smoke generation and measurement hardware were performed concurrent with and after the flights. Once it was established that the smoke produced in low gravity did not differ substantially from what was produced in normal gravity, the SAME hardware was used in additional ground-based tests with a research-grade aerosol instrument to measure the particle size distributions of smoke from the five spacecraft materials [4,5].

1.1. Spacecraft Fire Detector Background

Prior spacecraft fire detection systems have been discussed in detail in papers by Friedman and Urban [6,7]. In the early years of manned space flight, the Mercury, Gemini and Apollo missions had small crew habitable volumes and mission durations were short, consequently the fire detection design depended upon human senses of the crew to detect fires. The Skylab module in the 1970s, used approximately 30 UV-sensing fire detectors [6] which were limited to line-of-sight and were reported to have difficulties with false alarms. In subsequent decades, the Space Shuttle detectors were based upon ionization fire detector technology, which was the most advanced technology available at the time. An inertial separator was designed to eliminate particles larger than 1 to 2 μm from entering the particle sensing volume. The International Space Station (ISS) smoke detectors use near-IR forward light scattering, which is more sensitive to particles larger than 0.3 μm.

As described by Friedman [6], there were six overheat and failed component events in the NASA Space Shuttle fleet during its operational lifetime. Several similar incidents have occurred on the ISS, which are briefly described here. An electrical ‘odor’ was traced to lamp on Service Module (ISS Expedition 10, March 2005), a smoke and solvent smell reported caused by smoldering polymeric bushing (ISS Expedition 18, September 2006), the crew reported a burning odor and smoke from the water recovery system (twice), and most recently, the crew reported a burning odor and smoke coming from a failed micro-pump at the back of a pressure suit [8,9]. None of these events spread into a real fire but as mission durations increase, the likelihood of failures increases. The worst fire event was the solid-fuel oxygen generator malfunction on Mir in 1997, which sprayed molten flaming metal and filled the cabin with smoke. Fortunately, no flaming fire has happened in a spacecraft cabin since that time, but there is still a critical need for improved understanding of spacecraft fires and improved detection [10].

2.0. Methods

An experiment was developed specifically to increase knowledge of the types of smoke that might occur in spacecraft and the effects of low gravity on smoke generation: the Smoke Aerosol Measurement Experiment (SAME). The goal of SAME was to generate repeatable smoke and obtain particle size statistics on-orbit without relying exclusively upon sample return to Earth. This is challenging because existing aerosol instruments are typically large and incompatible with spacecraft experiment constraints. As will be described below, an alternative approach was employed that used three discrete instruments to measure separate moments of the size distribution. When combined, these moments provide useful aggregate statistics of the size distribution. The measurements were made using smoke generated by overheated spacecraft materials with rigorously controlled sample temperature, flow field, and particle aging time. The experiment flew twice, the first time in 2007 (SAME-1) and the second (SAME-2) in 2010. When discussion applies to both flights, this paper will refer to “SAME.” If the discussion is specific to a particular flight, the flight number will be identified e.g., “SAME-2.”

2.1. Moment Method

The approach used by the SAME experiment is termed the ‘moment method’ for convenience [11]. As will be described below, the approach consists of measuring three moments of the size distribution (zeroth, first and third) and using the properties of the lognormal distribution to estimate the geometric mean diameter and the standard deviation.

The average particle size and an estimate of the width of the size distribution will be estimated from various moments of the size distribution. The number distribution, fN(D), is defined as

fN(D)=dNdD
(1)

where dN is the number of particles per cm3 with diameter between D and D + dD. The moments of interest consist of the number concentration, M0, the first moment M1, and the volume or mass concentration moment, M3 and are defined as

Mi=∫DifN(D)dD i=0,1,3
(2)

when i=0, the zeroth moment of the distribution, M0, equation (2) is simply the number of particles per unit volume. In the SAME experiment, this was measured using a condensation nuclei counter. The first moment, i=1, can also be thought of as the “diameter concentration” or integrated diameter per unit volume and is approximately proportional to the ionization detector moment (signal). For particles in the Mie scattering regime, particles sizes from 0.3λ to about 3λ (~0.2 to 2.0 μm for a red laser, where λ is wavelength), the light scattering signal is approximately proportional to the third moment, i=3 [12]. From these moments, and a measurement of M0 using a condensation nuclei counter, two mean diameters can be computed: the count (arithmetic) mean diameter D0.5 or D¯, which is equal to M1/M0 and the diameter of average mass D1.5 or Dm¯, which is proportionally equal to (M3/M0)1/3 . These calculations do not depend on any assumed type of size distribution, lognormal or otherwise. (The basis for the subscript naming convention for D0.5 and D1.5 will be discussed later). The lognormal size distribution is widely used for describing aerosols including non-flaming smoke particles because for most aerosols; the bulk of the number concentration is associated with smaller particles [13,14]. The number distribution fN(D) for the lognormal distribution is expressed as follows:

fN(D)=Nt(2π)1/2D ln σgexp(−(ln D−ln Dg)22 ln2 σg)
(3)

where Nt is the total number concentration of the aerosol (=M0), and Dg and σg are the geometric mean diameter and geometric standard deviation defined by

ln Dg=∫0∞ln DfN(D)dD/∫0∞fNdD
(4)

ln σg=[∫0∞(ln D−ln Dg)2fN(D)dD/∫0∞fN(D) dD]1/2
(5)

For the lognormal distribution, one finds that the various diameter definitions given above are related to the geometric mean diameter, Dg, via the equation [13,14].

Dp=Dg exp (p ln2 σg)
(6)

where p is a parameter which refers to the specific mean diameter of interest, i.e. D0.5 for the count mean diameter, or D1.5 for the diameter of average mass. Figure 1(a) shows a typical lognormal distribution for a Dg=1.0 and σg=1.6 and Figure 1(b) shows the influence of σg. For this distribution, the corresponding values of D0.5 and D1.5 are 1.17 and 1.39 μm, respectively. Using equation (6), one can relate σg to the ratio of D1.5 and D0.5 via the equation:

σg=exp(ln(D1.5/D0.5))1/2
(7)

By combining these three moments it is possible to compute three mean diameters of the size distribution, Dg, D0.5 and D1.5, and the geometric standard deviation. Validation of this approach is discussed in Cleary, Weinert and Mulholland [11] and in Meyer et al. [4]. These statistical calculations provide more detailed information of smoke characteristics, augmenting the three basic measurements and providing a strong basis for design of spacecraft smoke detectors.

2.2. Instruments

SAME flight and ground test experiment measurements were made using three commercial instruments which had been hardened and re-packaged for space flight. Two are industrial and indoor air quality instruments and one is a residential smoke detector. These instruments were selected early in the project when there were few choices for commercial-off-the-shelf high fidelity miniaturized aerosol measurement options. Choices were ultimately made based on the instruments’ simplicity, low power needs, small size, and adaptability to spacecraft environments. Unfortunately, they all show material or size-dependent behavior.

The zeroth moment instrument is a P-Trak™ (TSI, Shoreview, MN, USA) which is a condensation particle counter that was modified for use in space because the isopropanol condensate does not flow downwards to the wick in low-gravity [7]. To mitigate this issue, very small grooves were added to the walls of the condensing section of the device to improve conductance of the condensate back to the wick. These changes were tested in a separate space experiment with good results indicating the modified device could be used successfully in low gravity. A wide range of dilution was required for the different smoke aerosols generated, so a device called a Dynamic Diluter was developed to ensure the P-Trak™ would not saturate. Details can be found in reference [4].

The first moment instrument is the ionization chamber from a residential smoke detector calibrated to give an estimate of the first moment. This device uses an americium (241Am) alpha-particle emitter source to generate ions in a region within a DC electric field. Drifting ions in the electric field generate a current, and the presence of aerosol particles reduces the current as a result of the attachment of the ions to the particles. The mobility of the charged aerosol is too small for it to be collected on the ionization chamber electrode. For an adequate signal to noise ratio the requisite minimum particle concentrations are on the order of 105 particles/cm3 and no sample dilution is required.

The SAME third moment instrument is the DustTrak™ (TSI, Shoreview, MN, USA) which is a nephelometer using a 90° light scattering signal with a wide acceptance angle and output calibrated to quantify the aerosol mass concentration of Arizona Test Dust (ISO 12103–1). Material-specific calibrations and corrections were needed to account for the range of particle sizes, shapes and refractive indices in the SAME experiment, and the particle density was required to compute M3 from the mass concentration. While some studies have shown that the DustTrak™ response is not generally proportional to mass [15,16], after applying calibration factors, the DustTrak™ was found to correlate well with the mass concentration. The calibration factors with uncertainty are given in Mulholland et al. [5]. The DustTrak has removable inlets that can remove particles above a certain size by impaction. This process is illustrated in Figures 2(a) and (b), where the particle-laden flow enters a nozzle followed by an impactor plate. The large particles are not able to follow the flow streamlines and impact on the plate and are thus removed from the flow (Figure 2(a)). The smaller particles in the flow are able to follow the streamlines and bypass the impactor plate (Figure 2(b)). The SAME-1 experiment included two devices, one with a 1 μm impactor and the other with a 10 μm impactor. The difference between the output of these two devices indicates what fraction of the smoke particles are above 1 μm.

A schematic of the SAME hardware appears in Figure 3. Space experiments are ideally autonomous, with minimal astronaut intervention beyond the initial assembly. Hardware with programmable experimental parameters decreases crew training requirements and increases the quantity and reliability of resulting data. Software controlled all aspects of the experiment once the crew inserted the fuel sample carousel and commenced the test sequence. For the space experiments, smoke was generated by heating a small sample of material from one carousel segment in the smoke generation duct for approximately 60 sec. During this interval, a rising piston drew smoke into a 6 liter aging chamber where it could be held for a predetermined aging duration, allowing the particles to coagulate. Half of the smoke was pushed by the piston into the moment instruments almost immediately for unaged smoke measurements. After a period of aging, the remaining smoke was measured. Additional information on the sample heating sequence is given in Mulholland et al. [5].

Schematic of the SAME hardware.

The SAME hardware system was installed in the Microgravity Science Glovebox (MSG), an ISS facility that provides many resources including: power, data, video, uplink commanding, and containment of any smoke particles generated (to protect the air quality in the cabin).

A small cylindrical sample of material (approximately 0.5 cm diameter and 1.3 cm long) was heated in the smoke generation duct for approximately 60 sec. A stainless steel heating wire was wrapped around the sample according to fuel-specific drawing specifications.. As current was applied to the wire, its resistivity was monitored with a feedback control system to maintain constant resistance throughout the heating duration. These resistances were correlated to wire temperatures and were maintained within a few degrees Celsius of target values, which varied according to the fuel sample material. During this interval, air flow was induced by a rising piston in the aging chamber which drew the smoke into the chamber. The smoke was held in the chamber for a predetermined aging duration, after which the smoke was pushed by the piston into the diagnostics duct for measurement by the moment instruments. Space shuttle and ISS smoke detectors were exposed to smoke in the duct. In order to determine smoke morphology, microscopy samples were included in the experiment design. The collection method was thermophoresis, which uses a large thermal gradient within a small gap (generated by a heated nichrome wire) to exert a net force on the particle towards the cold surface where the TEM grid is positioned (Figure 4). Thermal precipitators can collect a range of different particle sizes, depending on the design [17], and the SAME thermal precipitator sampled the smoke particles directly from the aging chamber, depositing them on a Transmission Electron Microscope (TEM) grid. After the return of the experiment hardware to Earth, the grids were removed from the assembly and examined in a TEM to obtain an independent assessment of particle sizes and morphologies.

3.0. Results and Discussion

Over 100 samples were tested, comprised of 6 materials commonly used in the spacecraft cabin. The fuels were Teflon™, Pyrell™ (a polyurethane foam), cellulose in the form of cotton lamp wick, Kapton™, silicone rubber, and dibutyl-phthalate (DBP) deposited on a porous wick. The test conditions included multiple sample temperatures and air flow rates. The baseline air flow velocity was 8.1 cm/s with runs conducted at rates down to 2 cm/s with a limited set conducted with no airflow while the sample was heated. The sample baseline temperatures for each material were selected to produce 1 to 2 mg of sample mass loss in 60 sec of heating and have reasonable signal on each of the moment instruments. To examine the effect of sample temperature, additional higher temperature conditions were also selected that would not saturate the moment instruments. The TEM grids recovered from the thermal precipitators on SAME-1 were found to be contaminated and unusable. For SAME-2 more rigorous assembly controls were followed and numerous good particle images were obtained. For typical runs, the particle concentrations were a few million particles per cm3 and the mass concentrations were 2 to 15 mg/m3; however, there were runs with fewer particles.

3.1. Moment Instrument Results

Geometric mean, count mean, and average mass diameter results from the moment instruments for baseline runs are presented in Table 1 [4]. As described above, calculation of the count mean diameter and the diameter of average mass is independent of the nature of the size distribution; whereas the assumption of a lognormal distribution is implicit in the calculation of the geometric mean diameter and the geometric standard deviation (σg). This assumption was explored in ground tests using SAME hardware [4] with the conclusion that it is reasonably valid for the spherical smoke particles generated from Kapton and lamp wick. Aside from the experiments, one can observe from the Table 1 that the distributions are somewhat compatible with the lognormal assumption. The geometric standard deviations are all physically reasonable (i.e., greater than 1, Eq. 3, and less than approximately 3.5 which is the practical limit on the magnitude of such condensation aerosol geometric standard deviations). The aging results show a substantial increase in the observed diameters. As expected, the increase in the count mean diameter is greater than the corresponding increase in the diameter of average mass. This can be understood since aging by agglomeration or coagulation consists of particle collisions which join two particles which would maintain a relatively constant mass of smoke over the aging duration while the diameters of the particles grow during that same period. For a polydisperse aerosol, these collisions usually involve the collision of one of the smallest (high mobility) particles with the largest (high cross-section) particles. This collision has little effect on the size of the large particle but it removes the smaller particles from the size distribution. This has a larger effect on the count mean diameter since it is linear in diameter while the diameter of average mass varies with the cube root of the diameter. Theoretical predictions suggest that this aging process will produce a near lognormal distribution and that the standard deviation will tend toward a self-preserving distribution with a value of 1.32 [18].

Overall, the Teflon and Kapton smoke particles were smallest. The lamp wick, Pyrell, and silicone smoke particles exhibited substantially larger diameters of average mass, particularly for the aged cases. This is further supported by Figure 5 which presents the ratio of the signals between the DustTraks with the 10 μm and the 1 μm impactors. In one case, the smoke goes thru the 10 μm impactor and the smoke particles that are less than 10 μm in diameter pass through to the DustTrak. In the second case, all smoke particles greater than 1 μm diameter are collected by the impactor and the smoke particles smaller than 1 μm diameter pass through to the DustTrak. As shown in Figure 5, lamp wick and silicone exhibit larger portions of the particle distribution (~ 40% of the mass) larger than 1 μm compared to 20% of the mass for Teflon and Kapton. The comparatively smaller diameters for the Kapton and Teflon will make detection of these smokes challenging for light scattering devices. On the other hand, the large sizes seen with the lamp wick and silicone would generate very large signals on a light scattering system. As discussed by Urban et al. [19,20], some level of particle size discrimination is generally required to have a system that is sensitive enough to trigger an alarm for the low signal from small particles such as Teflon and Kapton without excessive nuisance alarms from large dust particles. This suggests that detection of these particles separate from the background environment will be facilitated by a detection system capable of measuring more than one moment of the particle size distribution.

The increased residence time afforded in the higher concentration zone at lower air speeds is expected to increase the particle size. This is supported by Figure 6 which presents the diameter of average mass for all materials with five different duct flow velocities. This figure shows a strong trend of increased particle size at low flow rates with the greatest increase for Silicone and Pyrell smoke. This indicates that smoke particles trapped in an avionics enclosure in a spacecraft or in poorly ventilated region can be expected to have substantially larger diameters than smoke released in well ventilated areas. In the most poorly ventilated areas (e.g., avionics enclosures with no cooling air) the smoke can be retained at a relatively high concentration until it escapes. This effect was simulated by varying the aging time over 30 sec to 2000 sec in the SAME aging chamber. The diameter of average mass is plotted against the aging time in Figure 7. The diameter is seen to consistently increase with aging time. This effect can be substantial, producing an increase in the diameter from 55 to 100%. More discussion of this effect is presented in the TEM image section and in Mulholland et al. [5].

The tests conducted in normal gravity with SAME hardware duplicating the baseline flight conditions allow for a comparison with flight results. Nominal conditions were 8.1 cm/s air speed, fuel temperatures generating 1 to 2 mg of smoke in 60 sec and aging for 720 sec. The diameter of average mass from low-gravity is plotted versus the results from corresponding normal gravity tests under the same flow, temperature, and aging conditions and shown in Figure 8. Overall the results correlate strongly with a slope of 1. This suggests that at flow velocities of 8.1 cm/s or higher, which is in the range of current spacecraft ventilation flows, smoke particles generated in normal gravity experiments are roughly the same sizes as those generated in low-gravity. The potential error of this analogy can be estimated using Figure 6 which suggests that lower flow rates could increase the diameter of average mass by as much as a factor of 2.5. At low or zero flow, the smoke in low-gravity will remain highly concentrated while in normal gravity the smoke will rise in a plume and become diluted. These different environments will influence the aging of the smoke, ultimately leading to different particle sizes. Nevertheless, these results indicate that normal-gravity smoke tests with proper attention to smoke aging time may be an adequate substitute for costly flight experiments.

3.2. Spacecraft Smoke Detector Results

Selected runs from the ISS experiment are summarized in Table 1, which compares particle dimensions and geometric standard deviations of the size distributions obtained from both the moment method calculations and the TEM analyses. The particle dimensions show that Kapton smoke particles are substantially smaller than the other materials with TEM diameters of average mass less than 0.3 μm. Since the ISS smoke detector is calibrated with 0.6 μm particles and the laser wavelength is approximately 1 μm, its performance can be expected to be diminished for particles that are significantly smaller than the calibrated range and the wavelength. The data from the ISS and Space Shuttle smoke detectors are presented in Figure 9. There is a much smaller data set for the Space Shuttle detector as it was only operated intermittently because its required flow rate (driven by an internal pump) consumed three liters per minute of aerosol making it impossible to do aging tests. The ISS detector signal was frequently saturated mainly by the larger smoke materials Silicone, Pyrell and lamp wick. The data points below the voltage threshold boundaries are cases where the detectors would have failed to alarm. For the Space Shuttle detector, the materials in this category are Kapton, Teflon, and lamp wick, whereas the ISS detector had difficulties detecting Kapton and Teflon smoke. The Shuttle was somewhat less sensitive than the ISS detector but, for the conditions tested, performed better for Teflon smoke than the ISS detector. The ISS detector would not have alarmed for over half of the Kapton smoke tests, and 2/3 of the Teflon tests. The poor performance for the ISS detector for these two materials, is consistent with the reduced performance of light scattering for particles substantially smaller than the wavelength of the light source. However, this is a concern as Kapton and Teflon are very commonly used in spacecraft, especially in electrical systems where they can be expected to be among the first materials to overheat in a smoke-producing event.

3.3. TEM Results

Overall, particles were collected on 72 Transmission Electron Microscope (TEM) grids using the thermal precipitator. These samples were analyzed in a TEM to evaluate the particle morphology as a function of material, heating level and flow conditions. DBP presented no particles that were visible via TEM, presumably because the particles were condensed liquid that evaporated in the vacuum of the TEM. Similarly, the volatile portion of the silicone rubber smoke particles were affected by the vacuum in the microscope and only residue remained. Figure 10 presents typical images for Teflon, Pyrell, lamp wick and Kapton.

As seen in Table 1, aging did increase the average particle size, however this level of change is not readily visible in the TEM images, instead the most visible change is the disappearance of the smallest particles. This is seen in Figure 11, which shows unaged smoke particles above the aged samples of Pyrell, lamp wick and Kapton. As described in the discussion of Table 1, this is consistent with expectations since the smaller particles have the highest mobility and consequently readily agglomerate with larger particles. Particularly for the semi-liquid particles, this combination of the smallest particles with larger particles is expected to have little or no apparent effect on the size of the larger particles because the diameter of a sphere is the cube root of the volume. In the case of non-liquid particles, the smallest particles are difficult to discern via TEM when they are attached to larger particles. Impact of flow on particle morphology

In the SAME setup, as the samples are heated by the wire coil, chemical bonds are broken and gaseous products are released via pyrolysis. These products may include partially oxidized species resulting from the reaction with oxygen. As the gases escape the sample, the temperature and species concentration will decrease as a result of conduction/diffusion and convection. With the decrease in temperature, the super-saturation ratio of the pyrolysis gas will pass a critical stage and nucleation of smoke particles will occur. These smoke nuclei grow rapidly as pyrolysis products condense on them, where the time scale for the nucleation and condensation is on the order of a millisecond or less. Over a longer time scale, the growing smoke particles in Brownian motion collide and stick together in a process known as coagulation. This process may continue for time scales of up to 103 s. Additional details on thermal decomposition of individual fuels can be found in Mulholland et al. [5] and Meyer [21]. If the particles are liquid, the colliding particles will coalesce and form larger droplets. This is the case for the lamp wick and Kapton smokes (Fig. 11(b) and (c)). If the particles are solid, they will retain their structure as they stick resulting in an agglomerate structure composed of many primary particles connected in a low density fractal-like structure. This is the case for the Teflon (Fig. 10(a)) and Pyrell smokes (Fig. 11(a)).

As the wire temperature increases, more of the sample will be pyrolyzed and these gases will subsequently condense on the nuclei leading to a larger mass concentration of the aerosol and a larger diameter of average mass. Key parameters affecting the properties of the smoke produced here are the convective flow past the heated wire, the wire temperature, the aging time and the low-gravity condition.

A case of special interest is the test conditions with the absence of flow through the smoke generation duct referred to as zero-flow in this paper (duct shown in Figure 3). This condition is of special interest to low-gravity because the lack of buoyancy and absence of a convective flow limits smoke transport to molecular diffusion and thermal expansion. Unfortunately, the zero-flow condition was not in the original design of the SAME hardware but was enabled later in the project by modifying the valve operation in the SAME hardware. This produced the desired result of a very high concentration of particles in which the low gravity aging process was enhanced compared to that in the aging chamber at dilute conditions. The design was such that the flow forced the smoke in the direction of the aging chamber, and unfortunately, the absence of flow allowed the smoke to escape through the sample carousel (Fig. 3). A side effect was that approximately 2/3 of the smoke was lost from the system because it was transported by diffusion and thermal expansion out of the duct inlet into the glovebox containing the SAME hardware. As a result, the total particle counts were lower than typical runs and the TEM grids had few particles. Eleven tests were performed with the zero-flow condition.

The thermal expansion induced smoke-flow observed with a video camera ranged from 0.5 cm/s to 1.5 cm/s with the higher velocity corresponding to Kapton and Teflon with the highest pyrolysis temperatures. The vapor concentration is inversely proportional to the flow so the nominal vapor concentration for the zero-flow case is about eight times higher than for the standard 8.1 cm/s forced flow condition. The increased vapor concentration causes increased growth of nucleating particles from condensation and coagulation as the vapor cools. The diameter of average mass is a factor of 1.7 larger for the zero-flow compared to the standard flow condition. This is an average for all the materials, which vary from as low as 1.3 for Teflon to 2.8 for Kapton.

For the zero-flow case, the heat is transported primarily by conduction rather than convection. This is likely to result in a significant increase in the gas temperature relative to the case with 8.1 cm/s flow velocity. The striking effect of this temperature change is the change in size of the coalesced particles compared to the flow cases, shown in Figure 12. Note that the morphology of the Pyrell smoke changed from fractal-like clusters with up to 30 primary spheres for the case with a flow of 8.1 cm/s to much larger individual spherical particles for the zero-flow condition (Fig. 12(a) and (b)). It is important to recognize that this zero-flow condition is unique to low-gravity scenarios. If one performed the same experiment at 1-g, the buoyancy of the rising gases would greatly increase the mixing zone. So the larger particle diameters observed in these zero-flow conditions are important to consider in the development of improved fire detection equipment for space vehicles.

4.0. Conclusions

The SAME experiment produced repeatable measurements of smoke particles from five materials typically found in spacecraft. From these results the following observations can be made:

For the conditions of the SAME experiment, all samples produced significant numbers of sub-micron particles that are readily detected using an ionization smoke detector.

TEM analysis results demonstrated a variety of particle morphologies that vary by material.

Particle sizes ranged from 100 to 600 nm and the measured parameters were consistent with a lognormal distribution.

Consistent with expectation, particle sizes increase with aging.

The diameter of average mass for smoke particles increased substantially as air flow was decreased. The zero-flow conditions produced smoke particles that were, on average, double the size of those produced at the nominal 8.1 cm/s flow rate.

In zero-flow conditions,Pyrell and lamp wick particles coalesced into large individual spheres rather than forming agglomerates, as they did at higher air flows.

Ground based testing at 8.1 cm/s showed particle dimensions very close to the flight results. This suggests that low-gravity smoke produced in flow conditions of at least 8.1 cm/s are well predicted by ground-based testing.

The ISS Detector, which is based on light scattering, showed reduced performance for Kapton and Teflon smoke particles, which are smaller than smoke from the other materials tested. The Space Shuttle detector, which is based on ionization detection, fared slightly better for Teflon smoke.

Spacecraft fire scenarios include an even wider array of materials and heating conditions, and the detection environment is complicated by spacecraft background aerosols which can be quite large. Therefore it is improbable that a single-moment device can be sensitive across the smoke particle size spectrum and resist false alarms.
