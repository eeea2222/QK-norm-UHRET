In the pursuit of human space exploration, the safety of the crew is paramount. Environmental Control and Life Support (ECLS) Systems are a unique aspect of human spaceflight that keep humans alive and comfortable on missions. Properly bounding the myriads of potential scenarios and contingencies is the first step to solving them with complex ECLS hardware and concepts of operations. While low-Earth orbit (LEO) missions are becoming commonplace, the lunar orbiter Habitation and Logistics Outpost (HALO) has different mission safety challenges. Fire detection was identified early in the program as an area requiring special focus. Though there are multiple ways to approach fire detection, sensing smoke particles is the most common technique both in terrestrial and space environments. However, smoke detection in microgravity is not simple. It is a multi-faceted problem dependent on numerous variables, and consequently there is no “one size fits all” answer to the problem. Unfortunately, there was no official guidance or published standard from the National Aeronautical and Space Administration (NASA) to reference for the development of HALO’s smoke detection solution. The NASA Glenn Research Center (GRC) has performed experiments overheating common spacecraft materials, and the published data provided a path to analyze and shape the HALO smoke detector performance parameters. In particular, an alarm threshold analysis process was established in cooperation with GRC that considers the vehicle characteristics in conjunction with the smoke particle and toxic gas data. NASA standards do exist for human exposure to combustion products, and the Spacecraft Maximum Allowable Concentration (SMAC) tables were the criteria for determining when toxic gases and accompanying smoke particle concentrations were unsafe. The HALO smoke detection analysis approach can be adapted and applied to other space vehicles and habitats. Future NASA smoke experiment campaigns can augment and update the spacecraft smoke data sets and contribute to an official smoke alarm threshold standard but this paper documents an acceptable approach to use in the interim.

III. Assumptions Several major assumptions were made to apply the smoke data from GRC chamber experiments to the HALO vehicle. First, it was necessary to assume that all smoke particles and toxic gases are introduced into the environment
[...]
instantaneously. In reality there is a competition between smoke aerosol generation rates and vehicle air filtration rates
that results in particles being scrubbed as they are generated. If the generation rate does not exceed air filtration, the
SD will not alarm because the mass concentration of smoke particles can never build up to a detectable concentration.
At the same time, toxic gases in the smoke will continually increase in concentration if they are not actively removed.
This problem has been explored in the literature. 3-5 Instantaneous detection is assumed and there is no accounting for
smoke transport time. [...]
Other significant assumptions are that smoke and hazardous gas generation is the same for all fuel types in the data
and that there is a linear relationship between mass overheated and smoke generated. [...] Smoke generation in laboratory experiments is notoriously
unrepeatable, so similar runs are averaged.
The results from this alarm threshold approach are not hard rules but serve as a guideline to infer reasonable SD
detection limits. It is worth noting that the 2012 test campaign was intended to assess gas sensor performance, meaning
there is substantial variation in gas concentration results between instruments. Tunable diode laser spectroscopy
(TDLS) and photoacoustic spectroscopy (PAS) were the most reliable sensor technologies in the test campaign and
the resulting data were used in this analysis. [...] Finally, it is assumed that the material preparation
techniques in the WSTF experiments are representative of materials in the spacecraft cabin. For example, PCB
material will have different properties depending on configuration and how they are populated. Similarly, spacecraft
Kapton is present in thin sheets or tape rather than ground or cut in small pieces. These are limitations of the data set,
which is a product of the test objectives at the time. [...]

VI. Conclusion In the absence of a NASA standard governing smoke detection, Northrop Grumman and Paragon derived the HALO smoke detector alarm threshold requirements using the method outlined in this paper with NASA input. Though it is
[...]
based on many assumptions that cannot be substantiated, it does achieve the goal of reasonably determining an alarm threshold tailored to the HALO vehicle. As this approach is not perfect, there are several areas for future improvements. Future smoke test campaigns with newer spacecraft materials, incorporating newer processes and material finishes, should include multiple test points in low-pressure environments relevant for long-duration missions. Published reports of these experiments should include time-based variations in smoke particle and toxic gas concentrations. A large gap in the currently available data is the absence of space vehicle filtration, which has a significant impact on smoke detection. A larger-scale experiment campaign incorporating filtration as a variable will provide the opportunity to advance the current state of knowledge for spacecraft fire detection. These data would enable future commercial space developers to more accurately model the complexities that exist in detecting smoke in a human habitat, such as airflow and filtration rates. Resulting models can not only guide smoke detector threshold analyses but also inform optimal SD placement within the cabin.
