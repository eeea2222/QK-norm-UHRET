Towards Dust-tolerant Smoke Detection in Future Space Missions

54th International Conference on Environmental Systems ICES-2025-361 13-17 July 2025, Prague, Czech Republic

Marit E. Meyer 1 Northrop Grumman Systems Corporation, Houston, Texas, 77062 USA

Thorsten R. P. Schultze 2, Jan C. Balzer 3 University of Duisburg-Essen, Duisburg, NRW, 47057, Germany

Smoke detection in any space vehicle or habitat is fundamental for safety and mission assurance. If early warning of a fire scenario fails, the contingency may progress to catastrophic outcomes, including loss of crew, loss of vehicle, and loss of mission. Operational experience with smoke detection in spacecraft is currently limited to low Earth orbit, but space missions to farther destinations have different concepts of operations and more extreme environments that levy additional requirements on the hardware. Smoke detection on missions to the Moon and Mars will be particularly challenging as lunar and Martian dust will inevitably enter vehicles and habitats via human activities, despite the best mitigation techniques. Typical spacecraft cabin dust that is airborne in low Earth orbit has been an ongoing challenge, causing false alarms and requiring operational controls, such as turning off smoke detectors during housekeeping. This practice has been acceptable in low Earth orbit but should not be the norm for dusty destinations. This paper will present background information on smoke detection and airborne particulate matter in spacecraft, limitations of existing technologies, and scenarios for false alarms. Further discussion will look at future mission environments with more stringent requirements, including vehicle autonomy and cabin pressures. A previous paper introduced a smoke detector to prevent false alarms in lunar missions by smoke-dust discrimination (ICES-2020-125), and this paper augments the technology summary with engineering hardware considerations for future missions.

I. Introduction
[...] Increasing the sensitivity of smoke detectors in the spacecraft cabin may be a solution, however, this increases the likelihood of false alarms from typical spacecraft cabin dust. False alarms cause a host of problems for spacecraft operations, for example, on the International Space Station (ISS), the immediate automated response is shutting down ventilation and other systems, requiring hours of additional work for ground controllers to re-establish normal operations and document the event. False alarms can cause stress for the crew and disruption of important activities, but the long-term problem of frequent false alarms is the natural human tendency to take alarms less seriously. The tradeoffs between having a smoke detector that is highly sensitive to the earliest type of smoke versus having a smoke detector that will not alarm during occasional dusty events are difficult to judge. In fact, these two goals are mutually exclusive and cannot be achieved simultaneously with smoke detectors that are currently in space vehicles. A previous paper introduced a unique smoke detector that is resistant to false alarms in dusty environments by smoke dust discrimination. 2 This follow-on paper describes the continued work on this technology as well as requirements and engineering features for its implementation in future space missions to dusty destinations.

II. Background

Fortunately, a fire has never occurred onboard a National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA) space vehicle while in flight and therefore spacecraft smoke detectors have never been proven in an emergency scenario. The state-of-the-art detectors in space vehicles have come from the terrestrial aircraft industry. Over the years, research has shed light on the type of smoke that must be detected as well as the nuisance aerosols in the spacecraft cabin that are likely to cause false alarms. Additional background information on these topics will shed light on the need for dust-tolerant smoke detectors.

A. Current Spacecraft Smoke Detectors The space industry has never benefitted from a specially designed smoke detector to meet the unique environments and needs of low Earth orbit (LEO) or exploration missions. The potential market size for such a product has historically been extremely small, and custom technology was not justified. Space vehicles have used the same two detection techniques found in commercial off-the-shelf (COTS) detectors that are currently used in terrestrial homes, businesses, and transportation. The Space Shuttle had an ionization smoke detector, 3 which contains a small radioactive source that emits alpha particles. [...] The ISS has a photoelectric smoke detector, 3 which contains a light source and a photodetector. [...]

A Smoke detectors are basically particle detectors. In both descriptions above, the particlesthat cause a signal change and evoke a response are intended to be smoke particles, but they could also be dust particles, as there is no mechanism to distinguish the particle type in either technology. This is the cause of false smoke alarms—the wrong type of particle, namely a dust particle, enters the detector and steals ions from the current within the ionization detector, or a dust particle scatters light in the photoelectric detector. D. Lunar and Planetary Dust In future missions, another source of particles that will overlap with the size range of typical spacecraft smoke is lunar and planetary dust. The exact definition of dust vs. regolith varies depending on the field of study and there is currently no consensus on specific sizes that are considered dust. Park et al. characterized both size distributions and morphologies of two Apollo lunar samples for particles up to 20 µm. 18,19 The resulting particle size distributions have been widely used to show that a significant fraction of lunar dust is smaller than 1 µm. These sizes will interfere with smoke detection in the same manner that the typical ISS cabin dust causes false alarms due to overlapping size ranges. However, lunar dust hasthe potential to be present in much higher concentrations. [...] Turning off smoke detectors during vacuuming has been acceptable on ISS and could potentially be an operational control while cleaning dust from suits and air in lunar and Martian vehicles and habitats. However, in these scenarios, the time without active smoke detection would significantly longer than ISS, and thus is not optimal for long-term habitation on the Moon or Mars.

E. Smoke Detector False Alarms Reports of false alarms on space missions go back to the Skylab era, when the unique UV-sensing fire detectors were known to have difficulties with false alarms, followed by reports of Shuttle smoke detector false alarms, 20 with a total of 14 events (note that this total combines built-in test [BIT] failures and false alarms). Many incidents on ISS are known to have happened but are not tracked in publicly available material and thus difficult to document. Some examples of historic false alarms reported on the internet are false alarms in ISS U.S. Lab, 21 in the Russian service module, 22,23 and in the SpaceX Crew Dragon. 24 Historically, most smoke alarm events in spacecraft smoke detection have occurred due to interference from nuisance dust rather than from smoke. This is something to be thankful for, but also something that can be addressed. [...] These smoke particle sizes fall right in the middle of the size ranges of ISS sampled particles and those measured by the real-time particle instrument, from 5 nm to 3 µm. The ISS concentration spikes in particle concentrations coincided with crew vacuuming activities, which would have caused false alarms, except that the current protocol is to turn off smoke detectors during vacuuming to simplify operations.

F. Smoke Detector Inlet Features Note that any barrier that inhibits particles in the relevant size range from entering a smoke detector will prolong the time to alarm. The misguided idea that one can put a filter in front of a smoke detector to prevent false alarms from dust is based on lack of knowledge. A filter will capture smoke particles by multiple mechanisms (diffusion, interception, and inertial impaction), in the same manner that dust particles are captured. [...]

III. Smoke Detector Design to Avoid False Alarms A reliable method for reducing false alarms that is now also implemented in high-end terrestrial smoke detectors is the evaluation of scattered light measurements at two different colors / wavelengths, typically in the blue and infrared ranges. 26-29 This method exploits the fact that small smoke particles scatter blue light better than infrared light. With larger particles, such as terrestrial dust particles, this effect is small or non-existent.

IV. Features and Requirements for Exploration Smoke Detectors [...]

A. Environment Hardware for exploration missions must be able to operate in low pressure, for example, the Habitation and Logistics Outpost (HALO) lunar orbiter ambient environment will be nominally 10.2 psi (70.3 kPa) but must be able to operate in a range of pressures. An additional requirement is that hardware must survive vacuum in the event of a vehicle depressurization. Photoelectric smoke detectors rely on light interacting with particles, which is independent of the pressure of the surrounding air. However, ionization smoke detectors must ionize air molecules, which will be less abundant at low pressures. This changes the baseline current in nominal operation, so it is imperative to characterize the baseline operation of ionization detectors at multiple test points in the relevant pressure range of the vehicle. In current missions, dormancy is expected on the order of years, which may be challenging for COTS smoke detectors taken from the aviation industry. [...]

C. Features and Requirements [...] NASA Standard 1008 40 outlines predicted lunar dust loads in a vehicle or habitat after an EVA for in-space pressurized environments. V. Conclusion

This paper has outlined the need for dust-tolerant smoke detectors and described the current technologies that are prone to false alarms. The overlapping size ranges for target smoke to detect and measured common spacecraft and lunar dust particles show that false alarms are inevitable with heritage smoke detector designs. The frequency of false alarms is understood by communities working ISS and cargo vehicle operations, although quantitative information is not typically published. Some anecdotal incidents are documented in online space news outlets and are mentioned here. This paper offers additional information on the polarimetric approach for spacecraft smoke detectors to update the previous paper on the device, 2 including performance data in a mixed aerosol environment where dust is already present. Future tests with a revised and improved prototype are planned, focusing on its response to various types of dust, as well as pyrolysis smoke from a wide range of commonly used spacecraft materials and standard test-fires according to the European standard EN 54 for testing point-type smoke detectors. The tests will incorporate a newer lunar simulant and a spacecraft cabin dust simulant based on ISS vacuum bag dust. The requirements and environments described for smoke detector hardware on exploration missions show that COTS detectors from the aviation industry will not suffice as they have in LEO missions. Adopting dust-tolerant smoke detectors engineered for the space environment will improve safety for future space exploration to dusty destinations, which is a high priority for both government and privately funded missions.
